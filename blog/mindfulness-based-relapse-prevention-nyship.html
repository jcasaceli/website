Mindfulness-Based Relapse Prevention: Evidence and Practice for NY State Employees

By NYSHIP Detox Editorial Team · Published 2026-06-12 · Medically reviewed by Bradley Tourtlotte, MD

Recovery from addiction is not a single event but an ongoing process — and one of the most practical sets of tools developed to support long-term sobriety draws on mindfulness. Mindfulness-Based Relapse Prevention, commonly called MBRP, is a structured, evidence-based program that teaches people in recovery to recognize and respond to high-risk situations, cravings, and emotional triggers with awareness rather than automatic reaction. For New York State employees using NYSHIP benefits for addiction treatment, understanding MBRP and how it fits into the continuum of care can strengthen the foundation of lasting recovery.

This article is general education, not medical or clinical advice. If you or a loved one is in crisis, call or text 988, or reach the SAMHSA National Helpline at 1-800-662-4357.

What Is Mindfulness-Based Relapse Prevention?

MBRP was developed by researchers at the University of Washington as an integration of mindfulness meditation practices with cognitive-behavioral relapse prevention strategies. It builds on the insight that relapse often unfolds through a predictable chain of events: an internal or external trigger leads to increased craving or emotional discomfort, which leads to a habitual response — and in recovery, that habitual response can be a return to substance use. MBRP intervenes at the point between trigger and response, teaching participants to pause, observe what is happening, and choose a deliberate course of action rather than act on autopilot.

According to the National Institute on Drug Abuse (NIDA), effective addiction treatment must address not just the physical aspects of dependence but also the behavioral and psychological patterns that sustain substance use. MBRP targets exactly these patterns by building a practical skill set that becomes more accessible with regular practice.

The Evidence Base for MBRP

MBRP is not a wellness trend — it has been studied in randomized controlled trials comparing it to standard relapse prevention and standard aftercare. Research published by the University of Washington's Addictive Behaviors Research Center found that participants who completed MBRP showed significantly lower rates of substance use at follow-up compared to standard relapse prevention alone, with particularly strong effects on heavy drug use and craving intensity. The program has been evaluated across alcohol, opioid, and stimulant use disorders.

The Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA) recognizes mindfulness-based approaches as evidence-supported practices for both substance use disorders and co-occurring mental health conditions such as depression and anxiety. Because depression and anxiety are among the most common drivers of relapse, interventions that address both simultaneously carry real clinical value for people in long-term recovery.

The National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism (NIAAA) likewise recognizes that behavioral approaches — particularly those that build coping skills and distress tolerance — are a cornerstone of effective alcohol treatment and sustained relapse prevention.

How MBRP Differs From General Mindfulness

General mindfulness practice — apps, classes, or meditation retreats — can be beneficial for stress and overall wellbeing. MBRP differs in that it is specifically structured around the recovery context. Sessions focus on:

  • Recognizing the early warning signs of a relapse cycle — not just external triggers, but the internal states and thought patterns that precede craving
  • Urge surfing: using breath and body awareness to observe cravings as temporary waves that peak and pass, rather than forces that demand to be acted upon
  • Identifying automatic pilot — the habitual, unexamined mental mode in which relapse most often begins — and replacing it with deliberate, values-based choice
  • Building self-compassion, which reduces the shame spiral that can turn a lapse into a full relapse

This specificity is what gives MBRP its evidence base. The skills are not generic stress-reduction techniques — they are mapped directly to the high-risk moments that people in recovery actually face.

Core MBRP Practices

A standard MBRP program typically runs eight weekly group sessions. Core practices include:

  • Body scan meditation: a systematic, non-judgmental observation of physical sensations that builds the capacity to notice cravings as body-based experiences without acting on them immediately
  • Mindful breathing: a simple anchor practice that can be used in real time during moments of high stress or intense craving
  • SOBER breathing space: a brief, structured pause — Stop, Observe, Breathe, Expand awareness, Respond mindfully — that can be used anywhere, in any high-risk moment
  • Mindful movement: gentle, yoga-based stretching that integrates body awareness with the stress-regulation skills built in seated practice
  • Relapse prevention mapping: using mindfulness skills to identify personal high-risk situations and develop a deliberate, practiced response plan for each one

How MBRP Fits Into NYSHIP-Covered Care

For NY State employees accessing treatment through NYSHIP rehab benefits, MBRP typically appears as a component of intensive outpatient (IOP) programming or as a structured aftercare intervention following residential or inpatient treatment. The federal Mental Health Parity and Addiction Equity Act (MHPAEA) requires most plans, including NYSHIP and the Empire Plan, to cover behavioral health treatment on terms comparable to medical care — which means evidence-based behavioral therapies informed by MBRP are generally a covered benefit when clinically indicated.

According to MedlinePlus, substance use disorders are chronic conditions that benefit from long-term support and evidence-based management. MBRP is designed precisely for this long-term phase — helping people maintain the gains made in formal treatment by building a durable internal skill set rather than relying solely on external supports.

Getting Started

MBRP does not require any prior meditation experience — it is designed to be learned from the ground up, typically within a structured group program guided by a trained clinician. Regular home practice between sessions is a key part of how the skills develop and become reliable in high-risk moments. Frequently Asked Questions

Frequently Asked Questions

Is mindfulness-based relapse prevention covered by NYSHIP? ▾
Evidence-based behavioral therapies for addiction treatment, including MBRP-informed approaches, are generally covered by NYSHIP and the Empire Plan when clinically indicated, under federal mental health parity protections. Specific coverage depends on your plan and provider network. Call 213-321-6518 for a free benefits verification.
Do I need meditation experience to participate in MBRP? ▾
No. MBRP is designed to be learned from the ground up — no prior meditation experience is required or expected. Sessions introduce practices step by step, and facilitators are trained to work with participants across all experience levels, including those who are skeptical of meditation.
How long does a standard MBRP program take? ▾
A standard MBRP curriculum consists of eight weekly group sessions, each typically lasting about two hours, often supplemented by brief daily home practice between sessions. Some programs offer condensed or adapted versions. Ask your treatment provider about the specific format available to you.
Can MBRP help with anxiety and depression in recovery? ▾
MBRP is specifically designed to address the emotional discomfort — including anxiety, low mood, and stress — that commonly drives relapse risk. Research and SAMHSA guidance both recognize mindfulness-based approaches as evidence-supported for co-occurring mood conditions alongside substance use disorders. It is a complement to, not a replacement for, clinical treatment of depression or anxiety.
